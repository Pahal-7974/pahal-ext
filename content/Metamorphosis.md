**Spoiler alert. If you haven't read Metamorphosis, turn back now *(or don't)*.**

I went into this book with a lot of context about the _author_ and zero context about the _book_. I'd visited Kafka's home and writing room in Prague two years ago, and I knew he was a famous philosopher-author type. So naturally I assumed _Metamorphosis_ would be some quiet, Plato-and-Socrates-adjacent meditation on the meaning of life.

Let me assure you, it is *not* that. (Spoilers ahead!)

> ## Wait, He's an Insect?
> 
> For the first twenty pages I genuinely could not process that this man just... became an insect. No explanation, no build-up. You wake up, you're a bug, and that's it.
>
>And then I spent the rest of it stuck on a much dumber question: **how big is this insect?** The text never says, and it drove me a little insane. My case for it _not_ being human-sized:
> 
> ** **The cane.** His father threatens him with a cane, and it actually works as a threat. A human-sized bug would not care about a waist-level cane, or even a rolled-up newspaper. This only makes sense if he's normal insect-sized.
> 
> ** **The portrait scene.** When his mother and sister try to clear the furniture and he clings to a portrait on the wall; have you ever seen a bedroom portrait bigger than a human? That scene only works if he's smaller than the frame.
> 
> ** **Getting stuck under the bed.** The sheer physical struggle of it getting out from under furniture reads like something kid-sized, not human-sized.
> 
> By the end I'd landed somewhere between "normal bug" and "vaguely kid-sized," which was no less terrifying, but it's Kafka's fault, not mine.
> 
> (Also: turns out it's specifically a **dung beetle**, and we only find out because the maid says so. Up until then I was just assuming cockroach, beetle, or an equally creepy kind of insect).
> 
> ## The Apple
> 
> The scene where his father throws an apple that lodges in his back and just... stays there, rotting, for the rest of the book? That one lives in my head now. Also didn't help my sizing theory: an apple embedding itself in something doesn't really work at either 'normal bug' or 'human' scale, which just reinforced my 'somewhere in the middle' theory.
> 
> ## Okay But Actually
> 
> Underneath the "how high was this guy" jokes (and I do wonder), the book is genuinely unsettling in a way that's stuck with me. I catch myself just staring at a wall thinking about it, which is not a normal side effect of finishing a book.
> 
> The thing that actually got me: imagine waking up tomorrow as an insect. Your entire life, just _over_, while you exist to see the consequences of your metamorphosis (*see what I did there? ;))*. That's the horror. Not the bug. The complete erasure of a person while everyone still has to deal with what's left of them.
> 
> The sister is the one soft spot in the whole thing. Her pity for him is the last thread of humanity anyone in the house extends, and even that runs out eventually. He dies slowly, ignored, and it only really registers with the family once _they_ start facing social ostracization for having a bug-son. Which says a lot more about the family than about him.
> 
> The ending is famously abrupt, and honestly, a little anticlimactic, though given Kafka reportedly wanted his work destroyed rather than published, maybe an unsatisfying ending tracks.

## Verdict

Weird, upsetting, and unexpectedly hard to stop thinking about. Would not recommend as a "light read based on a friend's recommendation" without a warning label. Would recommend if you want a book that rearranges something in your brain for a week.

**Rating: 4/5 dung beetles.**
